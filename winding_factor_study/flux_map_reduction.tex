\subsection{The estimator and derivative evidence}
\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small\caption{All-data constant factors in A/A. B uses fixed-q d-flux prediction; C uses joint flux prediction. The derivative LS column optimizes a different criterion.}\FactorTable\label{tab:factors}\end{table}
The conventional factors are 13.4921, 13.5184 and 14.7642 for Sets 1--3. Their difference from the earlier saved numbers reflects a fresh common preprocessing run rather than reuse of historical results. The fixed-q estimate B and joint estimate C are identified independently. Differences among A, B, C and the derivative LS estimate cannot be attributed solely to q-current drift: the objectives, sampled points, surface approximations and weighting rules also differ.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small\caption{Contour diagnostics. Centroid RMS is the d-flux difference between a contour's coordinate centroid and its selected level; it is not a reconstruction RMSE. The final column quantifies centroid q-current drift.}\ContourTable\end{table}
Nonzero centroid departure confirms that mean contour coordinates are generally not points of the same constant-flux contour. The drift term in~\eqref{eq:drift} is therefore not the only distinction between the implemented statistic and a fixed-q local derivative.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small\caption{Primary 80-neighbor derivative diagnostics. All residual norms are dimensionless ratios of inductance norms, not relative flux errors.}\DerivativeTable\label{tab:derivatives}\end{table}
The global derivative factors are approximately 13.84, 13.95 and 14.45. Changing from A to the derivative-optimal constant only modestly reduces the aggregate derivative residual. Even a pointwise joint factor leaves a nonzero residual. The primary normalized-direction medians are approximately 0.090, 0.087 and 0.144. This is evidence of imperfect collinearity in the estimated sensitivities, with larger directional disagreement in Set 3. It is not a calibrated physical percentage error.

The maps locate where separate groups ask a common table for different values. Improving the scalar factor can move and reduce some discrepancies, but a shared table cannot simultaneously reproduce distinct requested values at one coordinate. Pairwise incompatibility and derivative-direction disagreement are complementary evidence. They do not supply an isolated physical error floor because original-surface interpolation and measurement uncertainty remain present. The folding report records reversed projected facets and ambiguous query memberships; these queries are excluded rather than assigned an arbitrary surface value.

The pooled adjacent-surface disagreement for A is 1.868, 1.820 and 2.597 mVs in Sets 1--3; G changes these values to 1.800, 1.751 and 2.422 mVs. The larger Set 3 disagreement is qualitatively consistent with its larger derivative-direction diagnostic, while neither numerical procedure eliminates its own estimation uncertainty. These pairwise surface differences must not be subtracted from shared-table RMSEs: they use different queries and error definitions.

\ResultTables

The reconstruction rows include the entire storage-and-return operation. They use the source points again and therefore measure approximation of available data rather than prediction of new excitation groups. The prediction rows remove a full group, refit all parameters from the remaining data and then query the unseen group. These two rows of evidence must not be interchanged.

On the common interior prediction points, A has joint RMSE values of 5.998, 6.309 and 6.970 mVs in Sets 1--3. C gives 5.518, 5.736 and 6.996 mVs: optimizing a joint training criterion improves the first two sets but does not improve the unseen-group aggregate in Set 3. B also does not rank uniformly above or below C. These outcomes preclude attributing the remaining error solely to the conventional estimator or to moving q-current.

E gives 5.453, 5.470 and 6.839 mVs; F gives 5.490, 5.653 and 6.529 mVs; G gives 5.513, 5.443 and 6.425 mVs. E is the best reduced candidate in Set 1, and G is best in Sets 2 and 3. G's advantage over E in Set 2 is only 0.027 mVs, while G is slightly worse than E in Set 1. In Set 3 its advantage over the simpler F is 0.104 mVs, with a lower d-flux error but a higher q-flux error. The ranking therefore depends on machine configuration and on the equal-component aggregate used here.

H gives 1.787, 1.780 and 1.480 mVs, substantially below every reduced candidate on the identical points. The common prediction coverages are 89.15\%, 89.03\% and 90.75\% of the eligible interior points. The cumulative error curves are conditional on common supported points. To express a retained fraction relative to all eligible points, multiply the plotted fraction by the reported common coverage. Missing support is not a zero-error observation.

Their best visited coefficients are retained, but G's ranking is consequently a comparison of computed candidates, not a certified converged optimum. Across all 37 held-out groups, the smallest evaluated G Jacobian is 0.759; none is nonpositive or below the training margin of 0.25. This confirms the fixed-other-currents monotonicity check at the evaluated points, while the separate empirical excitation-graph folding check remains necessary.

\subsection{Boundary groups and unsupported extrapolation}
\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small\caption{Boundary-group prediction for representative methods. C/G use the reduced-model common support. H uses its own support and is therefore not a matched-support accuracy comparison with C/G. Excitation is nominal, in A. Boundary removal is more demanding than interpolation between retained excitation groups. H can lose all support when no remaining point surrounds the query in physical three-current space. Reduced coordinates may remain supported even though excitation lies outside the observed training range. The reported boundary errors assess only those available measured points; they do not validate unrestricted excitation extrapolation or an unmeasured operating range.

\subsection{Numerical controls and excitation-reference sensitivity}
\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small\caption{Scaling and raster controls. The four scaling rows use one common physical mask per dataset. The three raster rows use a separate common mask across the three raster resolutions. Counts are therefore not interchangeable between controls. Errors are in mVs.}\NumericalTable\label{tab:numerical}\end{table}
Raw and normalized axes can generate different triangulations even with identical source values. Differences between a normalized 3D reference and an unnormalized 2D reduction would mix coordinate-model and numerical effects. Here both are normalized in the primary comparison, and the four-way control makes the scaling dependence visible. Changing raster density quantifies the storage contribution for A without identifying a unique physical cause of the remaining error. RMSE differences between these rows are not subtracted to estimate a structural error component.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small\caption{Sensitivity of the A table output to replacing measured excitation by the nominal group label at the query. These are differences between two returned predictions, not measurement errors.}\NominalTable\label{tab:nominal}\end{table}
Within-group excitation scatter therefore has a concrete coordinate consequence. This calculation keeps the same shared table and changes only the query. It is separate from the fixed-reference convention of a relabelled historical single-group table. The source audit confirms the actual convention of each available historical export rather than assuming that every export already carries current metadata.

Three small analytic controls were evaluated directly. Moving between distinct physical points on a fibre gives zero discrepancy for an exact constant shift; the integrated variable shift agrees to about $1.1\times10^{-16}$ Vs. Replacing the integrated shift by $i_e k_{\mathrm{diff}}$ in that example gives a maximum error of 0.27 Vs. A deliberately incompatible example gives the same $(s,q)$ but q-flux values separated by 2 Vs. These controls illustrate the mathematical distinctions; their arbitrary scales are not machine-accuracy benchmarks.

\chapter{Alternative Identification and Representation Strategies}\label{ch:alternatives}

The preceding chapters establish several different limitations: a contour statistic can depend on sampling, a common scalar coordinate can be incompatible with both flux components, a finite table can introduce its own errors, and measurement uncertainty can distort all of these diagnostics. A proposed improvement should state which limitation it addresses. Improving an estimator does not automatically increase the structural freedom of its coordinate model.

\section{Robust contour identification}
\subsection{Length weighting changes the averaging measure}
If an interpolated contour consists of segments with endpoints $\bm a_m,\bm b_m$ in the stator-current plane, define segment lengths $\ell_m=\norm{\bm b_m-\bm a_m}$. On a straight segment, the coordinate integral is
\begin{equation}
\int_0^{\ell_m}\left(\bm a_m+\frac{\ell}{\ell_m}(\bm b_m-\bm a_m)\right)\dd\ell
=\ell_m\frac{\bm a_m+\bm b_m}{2}.
\end{equation}
The centroid of the piecewise-linear curve is therefore
\begin{equation}
\bm c_{\rm length}=
\frac{\sum_m\ell_m(\bm a_m+\bm b_m)/2}{\sum_m\ell_m}.
\end{equation}
Subdividing one straight segment without changing its geometry preserves this integral. This avoids the simple duplicate-point sensitivity of an unweighted crossing average. Disconnected components still require an explicit decision about whether their lengths should be pooled or their branches matched separately.

Length weighting does not force the resulting centroid onto a curved contour. The variance mechanism in Equation~\eqref{eq:variance-departure} remains, now with a different weighting measure. Noise in contour location, ambiguous branches and gaps in measured support remain relevant. It is therefore a more geometrically defined statistic, not a proof of unbiased physical factor identification.

\subsection{Robust locations and fixed-q comparisons}
A componentwise median or a geometric median can reduce the effect of isolated extreme crossing coordinates. For example, the geometric median minimizes $\sum_m\norm{\bm c-\bm c_m}$. It does not necessarily lie on the contour, and changing the number or extent of disconnected pieces can still move it. Robust statistics address a specified contamination model; they do not remove curvature or coverage effects without further assumptions.

Comparing at the same physical $i_q=q_0$ more directly addresses the interpretation of Equation~\eqref{eq:fixed-q-slope}. For each excitation level, solve
\begin{equation}
\psi_d(d,q_0,e)=\lambda
\end{equation}
for a consistent d-current branch. Differences of these d-values no longer contain a moving-q term. Existence, branch uniqueness, sufficiently large $L_{dd}$, overlapping support and reliable interpolation are necessary. A family of nominal groups with measured excitation scatter also needs a clearly defined excitation coordinate at the selected crossing. Replacing measured excitation by a group label silently changes the experiment.

A different geometric approach aligns entire contours. For instance, one can minimize a weighted distance from the shifted first contour to the second:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal E(\delta)=
\int_{\mathcal C_g}
\operatorname{dist}\!\left((d+\delta,q),\mathcal C_{g+1}\right)^2
w(d,q)\,\dd\ell.
\end{equation}
Turning the displacement into a factor additionally requires a defined excitation separation. Density weights, unmatched contour ends, ambiguous nearest points and branch changes affect the result. Symmetric two-direction distances can reduce one-sided matching bias, but do not supply missing contour coverage.

These alternatives are mathematical proposals here. The available conventional and diagnostic results motivate them; a new quantitative ranking of these contour estimators has not been performed in this manuscript.

\section{An optimized global scalar factor}
\subsection{Reconstruction fitting and fixed-map alignment are different}
For a candidate constant $k$, transform every training point by $(s,q)=(i_d+ki_e,i_q)$ and construct a matching common table $\widehat{\bm F}_k$. On a population $\mathcal P$ fixed before searching, a joint criterion is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-reconstruction-objective}
\mathcal J_{\rm rec}(k)=\frac1{|\mathcal P|}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal P}
\norm{\widehat{\bm F}_k(i_{d,j}+ki_{e,j},i_{q,j})-\ps_j}^2.
\end{equation}
Both components enter in Vs with equal weights. Replacing the mean by a sum gives the same minimizer when $\mathcal P$ is fixed. There is no component normalization that implicitly makes one axis more important.

The subscript $k$ on the table is essential: the model is rebuilt consistently with the coordinates. In a fixed-map diagnostic, the reference $\widehat{\bm F}_{\rm ref}$ stays unchanged while only the query moves. That asks how well a specified reference can be aligned, rather than how well a newly constructed reduction performs. The pointwise diagnostics in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} use such fixed references.

A finite grid makes Equation~\eqref{eq:scalar-reconstruction-objective} nonsmooth in practice. Changing $k$ can switch a scattered triangulation, a rejected simplex or a supported raster cell. A bounded search result is consequently a computed numerical candidate, not a proven global optimum.

\subsection{Keeping the evaluation population fixed}
Computing a mean only over whichever points each candidate happens to support creates a serious loophole: a candidate could improve its score by losing the difficult points. One remedy is a predetermined population and an infeasibility condition whenever a candidate loses any of those predictions. The recent consistent-global fits use a one-grid-cell interior support guard at the reference seed to choose such a population.

A different existing investigation replaced missing components by a 1 Vs residual while keeping its evaluation denominator fixed. This also prevents simply omitting points, but changes the criterion strongly. In the earlier constant fixed-map two-axis diagnostic, the missing-data contribution accounted for approximately 99.6--99.7\% of the total objective. Its selected factors primarily reflected the coverage trade-off. That diagnostic is therefore not presented as the practical consistent-global solution.

A fixed-population rule still requires reporting its population and the model's full coverage separately. It does not prove that excluded boundary regions are unimportant or validate extrapolation there.

\subsection{What the existing scalar comparisons establish}
A jointly fitted scalar and a contour factor can differ because they optimize different finite-data quantities, not because one is automatically a corrected physical turns ratio. The archived joint-scalar fitting used inner omitted-group queries rather than the recent all-data reconstruction objective. It also differs from the derivative criterion~\eqref{eq:global-derivative-ls}.

On the archived common interior prediction points, conventional scalar, jointly fitted scalar, two-axis and full three-current results are compared later in Table~\ref{tab:archive-prediction}. Joint scalar fitting improves the first two datasets there but does not improve the third. Eliminating a contour-centroid statistic is therefore useful as a change of objective, but is not a universal solution of the reduction problem.

\section{Two constant global excitation factors}
\subsection{The complete local condition}
Consider one common coordinate pair for both flux outputs:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-axis-coordinates}
s_d=i_d+k_di_e,\qquad s_q=i_q+k_qi_e,
\qquad \ps=\bm F(s_d,s_q).
\end{equation}
The two constants form $\bm k=(k_d,k_q)^{\mathsf T}$. At fixed $(s_d,s_q)$, the physical stator currents satisfy
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd i_e}\begin{bmatrix}i_d\\i_q\end{bmatrix}
=-\begin{bmatrix}k_d\\k_q\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For each output, differentiate along this discarded direction:
\begin{align}
\left.\frac{\partial\psi_d}{\partial i_e}\right|_{\bm s}
&=L_{de}-L_{dd}k_d-L_{dq}k_q,\\
\left.\frac{\partial\psi_q}{\partial i_e}\right|_{\bm s}
&=L_{qe}-L_{qd}k_d-L_{qq}k_q.
\end{align}
The necessary condition for exact reduction is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-axis-condition}
\boxed{
\begin{bmatrix}L_{de}\\L_{qe}\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}L_{dd}&L_{dq}\\L_{qd}&L_{qq}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}k_d\\k_q\end{bmatrix}.}
\end{equation}
This uses all six derivatives of the two measured flux outputs with respect to the three currents. Excitation-circuit flux and its three additional derivatives have not been independently identified here.

The scalar model restricts the compensation to $(k,0)^{\mathsf T}$. A nonzero $k_q$ adds a second direction through which excitation can be compensated. This differs from fitting separate d-shifts for the two outputs: both outputs in Equation~\eqref{eq:two-axis-coordinates} use the same coordinate pair.

\subsection{Local solutions, global constants and invertibility}
If $\det\Lmat=L_{dd}L_{qq}-L_{dq}L_{qd}\ne0$, matrix inversion gives the unique local compensating pair
\begin{align}
k_d^{\rm loc}
&=\frac{L_{qq}L_{de}-L_{dq}L_{qe}}{\det\Lmat},\\
k_q^{\rm loc}
&=\frac{L_{dd}L_{qe}-L_{qd}L_{de}}{\det\Lmat}.
\end{align}
A small determinant relative to matrix size makes this computation sensitive; conditioning must be checked, not just algebraic nonzero values. For singular $\Lmat$, $\bm b$ may be outside its range, giving no exact local pair, or inside its range, giving nonunique pairs.

Existence of a local pair at every point does not establish that one constant pair works everywhere. With a prescribed constant $\bm k$, the augmented transformation
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}s_d\\s_q\\i_e\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}1&0&k_d\\0&1&k_q\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}i_d\\i_q\\i_e\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
is always invertible and has determinant one. Discarding its third coordinate remains a projection: distinct physical points can share the same $(s_d,s_q)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:two-axis-condition}, together with the corresponding connected-fibre or intercomponent consistency condition, is locally and globally interpreted exactly as in Proposition~\ref{prop:scalar-factorization}. Invertibility of $\Lmat$ and invertibility of the augmented coordinate change are different questions.

Likewise, the pointwise inverse problem in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} can fit one known flux pair with two query coordinates. That does not require every point to share a common physical compensation direction, nor does it identify an online function of measured currents alone.

The recent two-axis global fit rebuilds the common $81\times81$ table for each candidate. It minimizes the equal-component squared flux error on the fixed populations 7093, 7693 and 8625 for Sets 1--3. Any candidate losing a member of that population is infeasible. Bounds are centered on the available reference pair, with several targeted starts and a finite search budget. These settings define a focused local numerical refinement.

\subsection{Measured excitation scatter can fold a projected group}
For exactly constant measured $i_e$, Equation~\eqref{eq:two-axis-coordinates} is a translation of each stator-current mesh. It preserves its signed triangle areas. A nominal group with an excitation graph $i_e=e(i_d,i_q)$ instead has Jacobian
\begin{equation}
J_{\rm graph}=
\begin{bmatrix}
1+k_de_d&k_de_q\\
k_qe_d&1+k_qe_q
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Expanding its determinant cancels the product term:
\begin{align}
\det J_{\rm graph}
&=(1+k_de_d)(1+k_qe_q)-k_dk_qe_de_q\\
&=1+k_de_d+k_qe_q.\label{eq:graph-determinant}
\end{align}
A negative determinant reverses local orientation. A small determinant indicates local area compression. Slight pointwise excitation scatter can matter strongly on narrow or poorly conditioned current triangles because its estimated spatial gradient can be large.

For a triangle on which the measured excitation is linearly interpolated, this relation describes the exact affine projected area ratio. The numerical geometry check nevertheless compares the actual original and transformed triangles and retains their original connectivity. No artificial links between groups are needed to produce a fold within one group. Overlap between different excitation groups is also not, by itself, an error: overlap is required if a common map is to serve them. Their demanded flux values must agree there for exact reduction.

\section{A general linear four-parameter transformation}
\subsection{Deriving the effective factors}
The investigated extension is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four-coordinates}
\bm s=A\bm i_s+\bm b i_e,\qquad
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&k_{dq}\\k_{qd}&1\end{bmatrix},
\quad
\bm b=\begin{bmatrix}k_{de}\\k_{qe}\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\bm i_s=(i_d,i_q)^{\mathsf T}$. The first index identifies the target coordinate, the second the source current. Thus
$s_d=i_d+k_{dq}i_q+k_{de}i_e$ and
$s_q=k_{qd}i_d+i_q+k_{qe}i_e$.

Let $D=1-k_{dq}k_{qd}$. If $D\ne0$, direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
A^{-1}=\frac1D
\begin{bmatrix}1&-k_{dq}\\-k_{qd}&1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Multiplying Equation~\eqref{eq:four-coordinates} by this inverse yields
\begin{equation}
A^{-1}\bm s=\bm i_s+(A^{-1}\bm b)i_e
=\bm i_s+\bm k^{\rm eff}i_e,
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
k_d^{\rm eff}&=\frac{k_{de}-k_{dq}k_{qe}}{1-k_{dq}k_{qd}},\\
k_q^{\rm eff}&=\frac{k_{qe}-k_{qd}k_{de}}{1-k_{dq}k_{qd}}.
\label{eq:effective-factors}
\end{align}
Conversely, $\bm b=A\bm k^{\rm eff}$ gives
$k_{de}=k_d^{\rm eff}+k_{dq}k_q^{\rm eff}$ and
$k_{qe}=k_q^{\rm eff}+k_{qd}k_d^{\rm eff}$.
This inverse relation provides a numerically transparent parameterization by two shears and two effective factors.

If a continuously selectable flux function is $\bm G(\bm i_s+\bm k^{\rm eff}i_e)$, define $\bm F(\bm s)=\bm G(A^{-1}\bm s)$. Then
\begin{equation}
\bm F(A\bm i_s+\bm b i_e)
=\bm G(\bm i_s+\bm k^{\rm eff}i_e).
\end{equation}
An invertible $A$ therefore adds a change of reduced coordinates, not additional structural freedom to remove excitation. Its four coefficients are not four independently identifiable magnetic compensation mechanisms.

\subsection{Finite grids do not commute with the coordinate change}
A regular rectangular grid in $\bm s$ maps to a generally oblique grid in $A^{-1}\bm s$. A finite interpolation operator on one rectangular grid does not generally give the transformed version of the interpolation on the other grid. Range normalization can also change the scattered triangulation and its long-simplex rejection. Raster alignment, finite support and interpolation can thus change reconstruction error even at identical effective factors.

The investigation uses the same 81-node resolution per reduced axis and the same linear scattered-field and multilinear-return conventions as the two-axis fit. Shears are bounded by $\pm0.35$, $\det A$ must remain at least 0.5, and its condition number must not exceed 3. The effective-factor bounds and fixed physical fit population come from the two-axis reference. Geometry constraints prevent new or deepened folds, and finalists with additional positive-area self-overlap are rejected. Existing measurement-graph folds are retained rather than smoothed away.

Table~\ref{tab:four-parameters} reports the best computed candidates. Their effective factors agree, to the displayed precision, with the two-axis factors. Several starts, including starts with slightly changed effective compensation, were explored. Some four-dimensional starts exhausted their evaluation budget, and different starts gave different nearby solutions. No global optimality or unique physical parameter interpretation is established.

\section{Full three-dimensional flux maps}
\subsection{What retaining excitation removes, and what it does not}
A full representation uses
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\ps}=\widehat{\bm F}(i_d,i_q,i_e).
\end{equation}
It does not require excitation sensitivity to lie in one constant compensable direction, or even in a common constant two-axis direction. It still approximates an empirical current-only relation; temperature variation, magnetic history and measurement distortion do not disappear by adding the excitation axis.

A regular-grid lookup uses eight surrounding corners per component for trilinear interpolation, rather than four for a bilinear two-coordinate table. This corner count is not a hardware execution-time measurement. The measured support must still be respected; a rectangular array can contain unavailable nodes inside its bounding box.

The stored full tables in the existing comparison contain $81\times81\times21=137781$ nodes, versus $81^2=6561$ for each reduced map. For two double-precision flux arrays, the value storage is
\begin{align}
B_{\rm 2D}&=2(81^2)(8)=104976\ {\rm bytes},\\
B_{\rm 3D}&=2(81^2)(21)(8)=2204496\ {\rm bytes}.
\end{align}
These are approximately 0.105 and 2.20 decimal MB, before masks and axis vectors. Twenty-one excitation grid nodes do not imply twenty-one independent measured excitation groups. They are storage locations sampled from the scattered field.

\subsection{What the archived prediction results show}
An existing whole-group prediction investigation removed each nominal excitation group in turn, refitted its model from the remaining groups, and assessed the omitted group. For fitted coordinate models, training contained smaller inner-group queries with a fixed missing-prediction penalty; the full three-current representation used the corresponding training measurements. The numerical choices and candidate hierarchy were fixed within that investigation. Its results are reused here without rerunning that campaign.

The full table is larger, its interpolation geometry differs, and all models use uncertain source measurements. When a boundary group is removed, a full three-current query can lose all support because the remaining excitation range no longer surrounds it. Reduced coordinates can remain numerically supported even then, but that is not a general guarantee of excitation extrapolation.

Existing grid-resolution and coordinate-scaling controls confirm numerical sensitivity. Consequently, neither the gain from a full table nor the difference between two RMSE values can be assigned exclusively to structural reduction error. Retaining excitation is a meaningful reference and a practical option when memory permits; it is not exact ground truth.

\section{Auxiliary computation and reduced online workload}
\subsection{Identify the actual dependencies before caching}
A larger map might be evaluated outside the fastest current-control update while retaining fast PI feedback and voltage limiting. This is an architectural possibility, not a measured real-time improvement in the present evidence.

The relevant input vector must be traced through the chosen operating mode. In the richer inspected controller, normal lookup coordinates use requested stator and excitation currents, whereas a mode selection can substitute internal measured stator currents. The simpler available structure also constructs its coordinates from references. These examples do not prove that every lookup in every mode depends exclusively on unchanged references.

Even if flux lookup inputs remain constant, rotational feedforward $\omega_e J\widehat{\ps}$ changes when speed changes. Resistance feedforward can change with temperature or its resistance estimate. Gain calculations can depend on resistance, dynamics factors and multiplicative settings. One must distinguish caching a map value from caching an entire voltage command or controller state.

\subsection{A conditional bound for stale map values}
Let $\bm\xi(t)$ denote all inputs of a flux lookup, and suppose the last evaluation occurred at $t_k$. If the map is locally Lipschitz with bound $M$ over the intervening supported region,
\begin{equation}
\norm{\widehat{\ps}(\bm\xi(t))-\widehat{\ps}(\bm\xi(t_k))}
\le M\norm{\bm\xi(t)-\bm\xi(t_k)}.
\end{equation}
If additionally $\norm{\dot{\bm\xi}}\le v_{\max}$, integration gives
\begin{equation}
\norm{\Delta\ps_{\rm stale}(t)}
\le M v_{\max}(t-t_k).
\end{equation}
At identical actual speed, the associated rotational voltage error is bounded by
\begin{equation}
\norm{\Delta\bm u_{\rm stale}(t)}
\le|\omega_e(t)|M v_{\max}(t-t_k).
\end{equation}
The assumptions matter: no jump in reference, valid support throughout, and a usable slope bound are required. A lookup crossing a missing cell or a reference step is not covered by this smooth-input bound. High speed tightens any allowed flux-value age for a given voltage-error requirement.

For reduced coordinates, the input change already includes excitation:
\begin{equation}
\dot s_d=\dot i_d+k_d\dot i_e,\qquad
\dot s_q=\dot i_q+k_q\dot i_e.
\end{equation}
Detecting only stator-reference changes would miss a changing excitation reference. A nonlinear coordinate map would require its complete chain rule.

\subsection{Scheduling, consistency and limits}
An auxiliary evaluator would need a defined update period or event threshold, a maximum result age, a consistent input snapshot and an atomic transfer of both flux components. Derivatives or inductances used together should correspond to the same inputs and table version. Otherwise an apparently faster architecture can introduce incoherent feedforward and tuning data.

The fast loop would still use current measurements, update PI states, calculate instantaneous speed-dependent terms and enforce voltage constraints. A stale lookup is a delayed feedforward contribution; it does not justify slowing feedback. Finite-difference derivative feedforward additionally needs a time base consistent with the held values. Simply reusing an old flux while differentiating a new reference can create a mismatch.

Worst-case execution time, scheduling jitter, memory access, data-transfer overhead and behavior during support loss would have to be measured on the actual target. No such real-time benchmark is available. A larger full map with a slower auxiliary lookup is therefore a candidate architecture whose accuracy-delay-cost trade-off remains open, rather than an established practical improvement.

\section{Further possibilities and the boundaries of this evidence}
A current-dependent coefficient introduces extra derivatives. If $s=i_d+i_ek(\bm i)$,
\begin{equation}
\dot s=
(1+i_ek_{i_d})\dot i_d+
i_ek_{i_q}\dot i_q+
(k+i_ek_{i_e})\dot i_e.
\end{equation}
Using the coefficient itself as a differential compensation slope would omit these terms. If a differential factor depends only on excitation, an accumulated shift instead satisfies
\begin{equation}
a(i_e)=\int_0^{i_e}k_{\rm diff}(\xi)\,\dd\xi.
\end{equation}
In general $a(i_e)\ne i_e k_{\rm diff}(i_e)$. More flexibility also raises inverse, regularization, support and identification questions; it does not turn target-informed pointwise factors into independently validated online functions.

Nonlinear coordinates, coefficient models, radial-basis representations and splines can change approximation and storage properties. Choosing a different basis is separate from choosing the input dimension. A flexible three-current fit does not prove that a two-coordinate reduction exists, and a flexible two-coordinate fit cannot represent two different flux pairs at exactly the same input. Preparatory work on such representations does not provide the missing comprehensive comparison here.

An energy-consistent representation could impose additional integrability and physical structure under appropriate assumptions. The present evidence does not independently identify the excitation flux and all associated derivatives needed for a complete three-current magnetic-energy model. These possibilities remain an outlook, not additional demonstrated solutions.

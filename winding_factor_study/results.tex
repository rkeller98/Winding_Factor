\chapter{Experimental Investigation and Reconstruction Errors}\label{ch:results}

The preceding analysis establishes what an exact reduction would require. The measurements now address a different question: how well do specific finite representations reproduce the available flux values? A measured flux field, an estimated derivative field, and a stored reduced table are different numerical objects. Agreement in one does not automatically establish agreement in the others. This chapter therefore states the target, reference map, and supported population whenever it reports an error.

These are observed channel ranges, not evidence that all points were measured at one controlled temperature. They also do not supply a temperature correction for each point. No unique decomposition into temperature effects, magnetic behavior, sensor effects, and interpolation errors follows from these records.

\section{From measured fields to a conventional reduced table}

Figure~\ref{fig:conventional-inputs} follows Set 2 from measured coordinates to conventional shifted coordinates. Both panels contain the same measured flux values. Only the horizontal current coordinates change in the lower panel. The visible movement is a coordinate transformation, not a correction of measured flux. If the reduction were exact, all excitation groups would agree wherever their shifted coordinates coincide. Apparent overlap is informative, but perspective and finite plotting resolution cannot establish exact agreement.

For every $\psi_d$ field view, the horizontal X axis is $i_q$ and the horizontal Y axis is $i_d$, or $s=i_d+ki_e$ after reduction. The $\psi_q$ views use the opposite current-axis order. The same component-specific order is used in the signed-error meshes and heatmaps. This display convention changes neither the physical current definitions nor the derivative-index convention.

The shifted points are merged into a common two-coordinate field. The stored representation uses an $81\times81$ grid. Scattered coordinates are normalized by their ranges before triangulation; coincident coordinates receive the arithmetic mean of their flux values. Grid values are obtained by linear interpolation within retained triangles, with long-edge simplices rejected. Queries outside the supported region have no returned flux value. A subsequent lookup interpolates the stored grid linearly in both coordinates. The full operation therefore includes reduction, merging, scattered interpolation, storage, and return interpolation.

Figure~\ref{fig:conventional-return} separates the merged representation from its return to physical measurement coordinates. A smooth common surface does not prove that the original groups were compatible: forming one surface has already replaced their differences by one stored answer. The returned group surfaces show how this answer varies over the original operating points.

\section{Error definitions and coverage}

For measured point $j$, let $\ps_j$ be its measured flux pair and $\widehat{\ps}_j$ the returned pair. The signed convention is
\begin{equation}
\bm e_j=\widehat{\ps}_j-\ps_j
=\begin{bmatrix}e_{d,j}\\e_{q,j}\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
On a specified supported population $\mathcal M$ of $N$ points, define
\begin{align}
\operatorname{RMSE}_d&=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{j\in\mathcal M}e_{d,j}^2},\qquad
\operatorname{RMSE}_q=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{j\in\mathcal M}e_{q,j}^2},\\
\operatorname{RMSE}_{\mathrm{joint}}
&=\sqrt{\frac1{2N}\sum_{j\in\mathcal M}(e_{d,j}^2+e_{q,j}^2)}
=\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{RMSE}_d^2+\operatorname{RMSE}_q^2}{2}}.
\label{eq:joint-rmse}
\end{align}
The joint statistic gives equal weight to both components. It differs by a factor $\sqrt2$ from the root mean squared Euclidean vector norm. Values below are in mVs; these are flux-linkage errors, not current errors.

Coverage requires an eligible population as well as a supported one:
\begin{equation}
C=\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{eligible}}}.
\end{equation}
An unsupported point contributes neither a zero residual nor an implicit successful prediction. A method can have an attractive conditional RMSE while failing to return values at relevant points. Fair accuracy comparisons use the same physical points and report their count. They do not become full-population comparisons merely because every number has the same unit.

Departure can exceed reconstruction RMSE without contradiction: the quantities compare different points with different targets. Nor does a centroid departure imply an equally large factor error. The factor depends on differences between adjacent-group centroid positions; shared departures may cancel, while changing departures may bias a secant.

An independent local calculation estimates differential sensitivities using weighted affine fits to nearby measurements in normalized three-current coordinates. The principal calculation uses 80 neighbours and requires more than one excitation group in each fit. Numerically ill-conditioned fits and insufficient d-current sensitivity are screened. This is a local regression estimate, not a derivative of the displayed reduced table.

With $\bm a=(L_{dd},L_{qd})^\mathsf T$ and $\bm b=(L_{de},L_{qe})^\mathsf T$, Table~\ref{tab:local-diagnostics} compares joint least-squares factors and directional disagreement. Here
\begin{equation}

\qquad

\end{equation}

Changing the neighbourhood changes numerical slopes and sometimes their supported population. A neighbourhood comparison must itself use a common mask. \section{Pointwise scalar alignment as a diagnostic reference}

How much error remains if every measurement receives its own scalar factor? Fix the conventional reduced map $\bm F_A$ and the measurement's $i_q$. For $i_{e,j}\ne0$, consider
\begin{equation}
k_j^\star\in\arg\min_k
\norm{\bm F_A(i_{d,j}+k i_{e,j},i_{q,j})-\ps_j}^2,
\label{eq:pointwise-scalar}
\end{equation}
restricted to supported table coordinates. The known target flux participates directly in identification. This is an alignment diagnostic on available data, not a predictor that infers $k_j^\star$ from currents alone.

As $k$ varies, the returned flux pair traces a one-dimensional curve in the two-dimensional $(\psi_d,\psi_q)$ plane. An arbitrary target pair need not lie on this curve. Even the best scalar factor can leave a nonzero residual in either component. Minimizing their joint squared error does not force either component individually to be exact.

The finite bilinear table makes this geometry explicit. At fixed $i_q$, every supported d-coordinate grid interval maps to a straight flux segment. Let its endpoints be $\bm f_0$ and $\bm f_1$, put $\bm v=\bm f_1-\bm f_0$, and parameterize it by
\begin{equation}
\bm f(t)=\bm f_0+t\bm v,\qquad 0\le t\le1.
\end{equation}
For a nonflat segment, differentiating its squared target distance gives
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\norm{\bm f_0+t\bm v-\ps_j}^2
&=2\bm v^\mathsf T(\bm f_0+t\bm v-\ps_j),\\
t^\star&=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
\left(\frac{\bm v^\mathsf T(\ps_j-\bm f_0)}{\bm v^\mathsf T\bm v}\right).
\end{align}
Comparing this candidate over every supported segment finds the minimum on the represented curve. The winning reduced coordinate $s_j^\star$ gives
\begin{equation}
k_j^\star=\frac{s_j^\star-i_{d,j}}{i_{e,j}}.
\end{equation}
A flat minimizing segment or separated equally good coordinates makes the factor nonunique. At zero excitation the factor cannot move the query; near zero its recovery is sensitive to small coordinate changes. Low excitation, weak sensitivity, missing support, and nonunique solutions are screened. These restrictions affect coverage and must accompany any reported error reduction.

\section{Pointwise two-axis alignment and identifiability}

Allowing two factors changes the attainable flux set. Fix a reference map $\bm F_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and write
\begin{equation}
\bm s_j=\begin{bmatrix}i_{d,j}\\i_{q,j}\end{bmatrix}
+i_{e,j}\begin{bmatrix}k_{d,j}\\k_{q,j}\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\min_{k_{d,j},k_{q,j}}\norm{\bm F_{\mathrm{ref}}(\bm s_j)-\ps_j}^2.
\end{equation}
This fixed reference is constructed with the separately identified constant pairs, in A/A,
\begin{equation}
(k_{d,\mathrm{ref}},k_{q,\mathrm{ref}})
=\begin{cases}
(14.336652,-0.025602),&\text{Set 1},\\
(14.186955,-0.091824),&\text{Set 2},\\
(14.627088,-0.787874),&\text{Set 3}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
These pairs define the reference; they are not the subsequently refined consistent-global solution.

For nonzero excitation, any supported reference coordinate pair is algebraically attainable by a factor pair. The flux target still need not belong to the image of the supported table. Within one cell that image is described by
\begin{equation}
\bm F(u,v)=\bm a+\bm b u+\bm c v+\bm d uv,
\qquad (u,v)\in[0,1]^2.
\end{equation}
Here the bold coefficients describe one bilinear cell, rather than the differential sensitivity vectors used above. Exact interior matches solve two equations for $u,v$. The coordinate-to-flux Jacobian has columns $\bm b+\bm d v$ and $\bm c+\bm d u$. Nonsingularity permits a locally unique inverse. It does not rule out another inverse elsewhere. Singular cells, multiple branches, and targets outside the image require separate treatment. Minimum-distance candidates include cell boundaries and singular loci as well as exact interior roots.

The diagnostic excludes low excitation, separated minimizing branches, and poorly conditioned recovered inverses. Geometric feasibility of choosing some supported coordinates holds for every measurement in this two-axis calculation, but differs from recovering an identifiable factor pair. Low-excitation exclusions number 280, 784, and 0; multiple-solution flags number 1566, 1602, and 2925; ill-conditioning flags number 11, 11, and 25. In these datasets the category counts account for all exclusions. No numerical failure is recorded, and retained two-axis populations contain 5619, 5695, and 6072 points. An excluded factor is not evidence of a large minimum flux distance: a target can have accurate but nonunique inverse coordinates.

The scalar diagnostic substantially reduces d-component error but retains appreciable joint error. This agrees with the curve geometry: one coordinate cannot generally place an arbitrary two-component target on its image. The much smaller two-axis errors demonstrate additional local alignment freedom. They do not establish an exact global reduction, a unique physical transformation, or an implementable online parameter function. Targets were already known, and some favorable alignments were excluded because their factors were not identifiable.

The distinctions can now be stated precisely. Centroid departure tests where a contour average lies on its original surface. Derivative residual tests local directional compensation. Reconstruction tests a map built from available data. Pointwise alignment tests how closely a fixed reference approaches known targets with individually chosen coordinates. Whole-group prediction instead removes a nominal excitation group before identifying a representation and querying that group's flux. Only the last experiment directly addresses unseen-group prediction, and even it remains conditional on support. Chapter~\ref{ch:alternatives} uses the existing prediction evidence to compare practical representations; none of the diagnostic improvements above substitutes for it.

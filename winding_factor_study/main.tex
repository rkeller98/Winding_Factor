\begin{abstract}
An electrically excited synchronous machine generally requires three current coordinates to describe its two stator flux linkages. A compact alternative replaces the physical d-current by a shifted coordinate containing excitation current. This manuscript develops the conditions, identification difficulties and control consequences of that reduction. It begins with a reproducible contour-intersection estimator and derives why a mean contour coordinate is not generally a constant-flux operating point. Cross-saturation, contour curvature, sampling weights and measured excitation scatter are separated mathematically. Exact reduction is characterized by directional derivatives and global fibre consistency; local differential factors, global fitted factors and pointwise flux alignment answer different questions.

Three measured datasets illustrate these distinctions. Conventional factors are approximately 13.49, 13.52 and 14.76 A/A. Their finite-table reconstruction errors, derivative residuals and contour-centroid departures have different units or evaluation populations and must not be interchanged. Pointwise alignment uses the known target flux and is a diagnostic rather than an independent predictor. Two constant axis shifts give an additional reduced-model degree of freedom. Four linear coefficients, however, reduce to two effective excitation factors and an invertible change of the stored coordinates. The observed approximately 0.5\% reconstruction improvement from this change is not accompanied by a consistent improvement on one omitted inner excitation group per dataset.

The electrical equations connect flux-map errors to speed-dependent feedforward-voltage errors, differential-inductance errors, PI disturbance rejection and voltage reserve. The controller discussion distinguishes inspected implementations from idealized design assumptions and from an unverified active experimental configuration. Alternative contour statistics, global fitting, two-axis coordinates, full three-current maps and slower auxiliary evaluation are assessed only after the limitations have been established. The conclusions distinguish mathematical exactness, finite numerical approximation and practical usefulness; they do not identify a unique physical source of every observed discrepancy.
\end{abstract}

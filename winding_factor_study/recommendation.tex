\chapter{Discussion and Conclusions}\label{ch:conclusions}

\section{What the conventional procedure assumes}
The conventional coefficient is the arithmetic mean of absolute-numerator d-current displacement quotients between adjacent nominal excitation groups at selected d-flux levels. Its coordinates are arithmetic averages of measured edge crossings. Its result therefore depends on the flux interval, group order, crossing distribution, edge filter and quotient weights. Nominal excitation determines grouping; measured excitation determines the coordinate differences and transformations.

Interpreting that statistic as a local magnetic compensation slope requires more than a clear contour plot. The local d-flux slope at fixed q-current is $L_{de}/L_{dd}$ under its nonzero-denominator assumption. An arbitrary contour trajectory contains the additional q-motion term. A numerical centroid can also lie off the chosen contour, so even that trajectory equation need not describe centroid movement.

\section{What is mathematically established}
The curved-contour example establishes a centroid departure proportional to q-current variance even when mean q-current is zero. It also establishes that equal centroid departures in adjacent groups can cancel from the factor estimate. Differences of second moments, not centroid departure alone, determine the resulting signed displacement bias in that example.

Unweighted crossing means are not invariant to changes in crossing multiplicity or asymptotic sampling density on a geometrically unchanged curve. This is a mathematical weakness of their interpretation as a unique geometric contour location. It does not prove the magnitude of that weakness in each measured dataset.

For one constant scalar coordinate, exact common stator-flux reduction requires both excitation sensitivities to equal the same scalar times their d-current sensitivities. These differential identities are locally sufficient under differentiability. A global statement additionally requires consistency along complete fibres, including equality between disconnected fibre components when they occur. Pointwise collinearity with a varying local factor is weaker than one constant global reduction.

Two constant axis factors relax the restriction to d-compensation alone. Their local condition is $\bm b=\Lmat\bm k$, but an invertible $\Lmat$ gives only a local compensating pair, not a proof that the pair is constant everywhere. The augmented linear current transformation remains invertible while its two-coordinate projection can identify physically different points. These notions of invertibility must not be confused.

Finally, a regular four-coefficient coordinate matrix is equivalent to two effective excitation factors followed by an invertible two-dimensional coordinate change. It cannot create additional continuous structural reduction freedom. A finite lookup table can nevertheless be sensitive to that change.

\section{What the measured evidence establishes}
The conventional coefficients for the three datasets are approximately 13.4921, 13.5184 and 14.7642 A/A. Their own-supported-point joint reconstruction RMSE values are approximately 5.03, 5.43 and 5.88 mVs. These values describe the complete finite-table return operation at measured points, not prediction at a new excitation level.

Nonzero centroid departures and q-current drift confirm that the conventional contour statistic is not simply a collection of fixed-q constant-flux samples. Nonzero estimated derivative residuals show imperfect local compensation in the empirical fields. Their magnitudes cannot be converted directly into flux-error percentages or assigned to a unique physical cause.

Pointwise scalar alignment improves an in-sample fit to a fixed reference but generally cannot match an arbitrary pair of flux components with one query degree of freedom. Two pointwise query coordinates can often approach the target pair much more closely. This improvement uses known target flux, and filters for weak excitation, nonuniqueness and conditioning explain the smaller retained pointwise population. It does not demonstrate an independently usable parameter model.

The recent consistent-global two-axis fits use a matching rebuilt table and a fixed objective population. They must be distinguished from the earlier fixed-map global diagnostic, whose large missing-data penalty dominated its score. The four-parameter fits preserve the fixed population and give approximately 0.5\% improvement on matched reconstruction points. Controls on their own fixed-fit common populations reproduce the selected gains without changing the effective factors. A single omitted inner group becomes slightly better in one dataset and worse in two. This is insufficient evidence to justify routine additional fitting complexity.

The archived multi-group comparison favors retaining the excitation coordinate within its supported interior domain: joint prediction RMSE is approximately 1.48--1.79 mVs for the full representation, compared with roughly 5.49--6.53 mVs for two global axis factors on the same points. This advantage is empirical and includes a larger table and different interpolation geometry. It is not a proof of an immutable physical accuracy floor for every possible two-coordinate representation.

\section{What follows for current control}
A model-minus-true flux error produces rotational feedforward error $\omega_e J\Delta\ps$ at identical arguments and speed. This term grows with electrical speed. Transient voltage prediction additionally depends on derivatives of the flux error; changing excitation can disturb the stator dynamics even at zero speed.

A stable unsaturated PI loop can compensate a constant additive voltage error with its integral state. Therefore a flux RMSE is not a direct current-error RMSE. Dynamic rejection, cross-coupling, measurement bias, update delays and voltage saturation determine the actual tracking consequence. Close to the voltage limit, extra feedforward demand can consume the reserve needed for feedback, and anti-windup controls the integral response to unavailable voltage.

The available controller examples verify that reference-based lookup, diagonal inductance tuning, optional differential feedforward, different voltage-limit structures and mode-dependent inputs are real design possibilities. They do not establish the active configuration or delay of the measurement hardware. In particular, neither ideal magnitude-optimum tuning nor a simple overall d-axis clipping priority can be assumed for every inspected structure.

\section{Practical choices and remaining questions}
A contour statistic is useful when a simple reproducible baseline is required, provided its sampling-dependent interpretation is retained. Length weighting or fixed-q comparisons address specific weaknesses but still require a fair measured comparison. Joint global fitting aligns the criterion with two-output flux approximation; it does not remove the scalar model's structural constraint.

Two global axis factors offer modest additional complexity and a simple inverse coordinate relation. A four-coefficient shear is useful for studying finite-grid sensitivity, but the measured benefit here is small and not consistent in the simple prediction check. A full three-current representation is a strong supported-domain reference when storage permits. Auxiliary evaluation could reduce fast-loop work, but its benefit requires target timing and delay measurements rather than an offline assumption that lookup inputs never change.

The unresolved questions are concrete. Which measurement and magnetic-state variations explain the empirical derivative mismatch? How much do contour weighting and matched-q sampling change factor identification on the same supported regions? Which controller modes and gain settings were active, and how do map errors affect measured transients near voltage limitation? Can a larger representation meet worst-case real-time deadlines with a controlled update age? The existing evidence does not answer these questions completely.

The distinction to retain is between an exact factorization theorem, an approximate finite-data representation and a practically useful controller model. The first needs mathematical assumptions, the second needs explicit numerical support and validation populations, and the third additionally needs a verified operating context and real-time performance evidence. No single numerical factor or reconstruction plot supplies all three.
